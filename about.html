Phase 1: Focused Mapping Review and Synthesis

We reviewed 15 publicly available guidelines that offer recommendations for inclusive trial design and conduct. Across these, we identified 183 individual recommendations and mapped them to the full trial lifecycle using the SPIRIT 2025 and CONSORT 2025 checklists as a framework. This produced the Q-ASSIST Trial Stages Model, our 12-stage framework of the trial lifecycle.

The mapping revealed a clear and consistent pattern. Existing inclusion guidance is heavily concentrated in the early stages of a trial. Later stages, including trial implementation and fidelity, data analysis and interpretation, trial oversight and monitoring, and post-trial access and participant follow-up, receive substantially less attention.

Phase 2: Revisiting the O’Cathain et al. (2013) framework

In Phase 2, we juxtaposed the Q-ASSIST Trial Stages Model with O’Cathain et al.’s (2013) framework of how qualitative research has been used within randomised controlled trials. This comparison confirmed the same pattern and identified three stages as particularly under-addressed, both in existing guidelines and in the published literature: trial oversight and monitoring, post-trial access and participant follow-up, and training and capacity building.

Together, Phases 1 and 2 show where qualitative research could do more to support inclusive trials methodology across the trial lifecycle, and they set the direction for the later phases of the study.

Phase 3: Horizon Scan and Mixed-Methods Survey

Phase 3 built directly on the gaps identified in Phases 1 and 2. Its purpose was to gather primary evidence from people working across the trial community, to understand whether qualitative practice at the under-addressed stages is growing, what practitioners are experiencing, and where guidance is most needed.

Horizon scan

We carried out a targeted horizon scan to see whether and how qualitative methods have been applied in the three under-addressed stages since O’Cathain et al.’s 2013 framework: trial oversight and monitoring, training and capacity building, and post-trial access and participant follow-up. We identified 29 studies across these stages. For trial oversight and monitoring, there is a growing body of qualitative work, using methods such as interviews, observations, and reflective logs to surface issues that quantitative monitoring does not capture, though the practice is not yet formalised. For training and capacity building, a recurring approach was the QuinteT Recruitment Intervention, which uses analysis of recruitment conversations to identify where communication breaks down and to guide targeted training, with several studies reporting improved recruitment afterwards. For post-trial access and participant follow-up, the gap remains largely unchanged, with what happens to participants after a trial ends still rarely examined.

Mixed-methods survey

We then ran an online mixed-methods survey of people working across the trial community, including clinical trial researchers, trial coordinators and staff, clinicians, and public and patient involvement contributors. Fifty-one respondents completed the rating questions and 42 also completed the open-ended questions. Across all twelve trial stages, respondents agreed that qualitative research has the potential to inform trials methodology and make trials more inclusive, with the strongest agreement at recruitment, support and retention, and at community engagement, and this view was shared across all role groups. At the same time, respondents rated the need for formal written guidance somewhat lower at several of the later stages, and their open-ended answers suggested that qualitative work at these stages was often not yet oriented specifically towards inclusive trials methodology.

Why we organised the findings into six domains

When we analysed the open-ended responses across all twelve stages together, the most important patterns were not confined to single stages but cut across the trial lifecycle. We grouped these into six clusters, each reflecting a shared challenge in using qualitative research to support inclusive trials methodology. Because these challenges span several stages rather than mapping onto one, we organised the findings into six domains. This makes the evidence base more coherent and gives the next phase six focused questions to work through rather than twelve separate ones.
